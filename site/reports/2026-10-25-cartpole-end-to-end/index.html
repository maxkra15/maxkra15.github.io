Reproducible learning experiment · 25 October 2026

Cartpole learning with stock and native CUDA learners

Stock RSL-RL PPO and the FlashSAC authors' implementation versus native CUDA backends, on the same Isaac Lab MDP and two distinct NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition allocations.

Abstract

Compared with stock RSL-RL PPO, native CUDA PPO used 40.7% less time on allocation A (4/4 matched runs faster) and 43.5% less time on allocation B (4/4 matched runs faster) to execute the full training process. Percentages use median paired time ratios.

For PPO on each allocation, 4/4 native runs and 3/4 reference runs passed the balancing gate at 400 iterations; 3/4 native and 3/4 reference runs passed at both 300 and 400 iterations.

Compared with the authors’ Torch FlashSAC, native CUDA FlashSAC used 47.5% less time on allocation A (4/4 matched runs faster) and 48.3% less time on allocation B (4/4 matched runs faster) to execute the full training process. Percentages use median paired time ratios.

Neither FlashSAC implementation met the balancing gate at any saved checkpoint on either allocation. Lower execution time therefore does not demonstrate faster successful learning.

Timing alone does not establish learning quality: the table below retains every seed's balancing and survival results. These are two hardware allocations with four repeated seeds each, not a statistical significance or noninferiority study.

What was measured

32 fresh training processes, 1,024 environments, 16 control steps per iteration and 400 iterations: 6,553,600 transitions per run. Every saved policy is evaluated at 0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 300 and 400 iterations on the same 128 deterministic initial states. Recipes are serial within each allocation, with Williams-balanced position and immediate predecessors within seed blocks. Cross-block transitions and compile-cache histories are not balanced.

The stock task ends at cart bounds or five seconds; a spinning pole can survive. The predeclared quality gate requires at least 95% clean time-limit completion and 95% upright time after a one-second settling period, with |wrapped pole angle| <0.2 rad. Early failures contribute zero thereafter. Full five-second upright time, all failures and active-step angle RMS are also reported.

Methods and limits

  • Current manager Cartpole task and Newton/MJWarp physics, unchanged asset/reward/reset terms. Actions execute through one shared clip at ±1, a disclosed override of the stock RSL wrapper default. Pole-fall termination is absent. Final observations are measured before automatic reset; later episodes never replace a failed first episode.
  • PPO uses the stock 32×32 ELU recipe and 5 epochs ×4 minibatches per rollout, matching the native recipe. RSL FP32 permits TF32; native PPO uses strict FP32 cuBLASLt and captured learning. FlashSAC retains the authors' residual/categorical architecture, uses one-step returns in both implementations to align timeout discount semantics, and performs two critic update attempts per eligible vector step. Its decay budget is 12,800 calls.
  • The pristine authors' FlashSAC uses compiled Torch FP16 AMP. Native FlashSAC uses FP16 dense with FP32 activations/master/adjoints/Adam, fused operations and the opt-in fresh-gradient path; autotuning is off. Intermediate rounding and stochastic trajectories can differ. Network/loss/update budgets differ between PPO and SAC, so cross-algorithm differences are recipe comparisons. Python3.12 is outside the authors' declared Python<3.12 matrix; their source is unmodified.
  • Initial weights match within algorithm and seed, across both allocations; optimizer/replay/scaler states start fresh/reset. Four seeds are retained without early stopping. Native checkpoint payloads were reopened on CPU, all scored checkpoints loaded from disk, learned weights were finite and updated, and actual optimizer attempts/completions/skips were audited. Controlled smokes separately exercise save/load continuation.
  • Full training-process wall includes interpreter/startup, asset and simulator setup, compilation/capture and final checkpoint writing. Complete evaluation-process wall includes its setup, seven checkpoint loads, rollout and output. Shared caches and smokes can precondition later processes; these are observed chronological costs, not isolated cold compilation trials. Installation/source transfer is excluded. Physics and learning are separate graphs; the Torch MDP is not part of a single fully captured graph.
  • Threshold intervals use worker-monotonic checkpoint write-finished times, with quality evaluated after training from saved policies. They are not online-evaluated success latency and differ in scope from the parent-measured complete process wall. No threshold crossing is interpolated.
  • Publication requires both actual workflow and comparison-task CLI statuses COMPLETED with exit zero, after verified whole retrieval and its acknowledgement. Completion-query and acknowledgement hashes are retained in the derived summary; successful comparison JSON alone does not establish workflow completion.
  • All clocks are observed, not locked. The allocations use distinct GPUs on the same node, so shared-host CPU and storage contention may affect timings. Concurrent jobs and allocation A's artifact bundling or retrieval can overlap B's remaining production and affect B's per-run wall. The same four seeds are repeated across allocations, not eight independent seeds. Two distinct devices do not prove broad hardware or task generalization. This is Cartpole learning evidence only; old synthetic or G1 timings are not substituted.

The Isaac Lab lock file is unchanged, with one explicitly preregistered runtime overlay: numpy==2.3.5 installed with --no-deps after frozen synchronization, uniformly for both allocations and every recipe. This resolves bootstrap dependency constraints; the installed runtime is not identical to the lock's NumPy selection. No fixture or learner ran before this amendment.
